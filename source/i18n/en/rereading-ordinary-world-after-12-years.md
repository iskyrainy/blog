## Memories of the First Reading
The last time I read this book was during middle school. Back then, Lu Yao and this book *Ordinary World* were so popular that every bookstore in our tiny, no-tier county town had copies for sale. During middle school, my dad would occasionally buy me some extracurricular reading materials, thinking that even if they couldn't improve my Chinese grades, at least they could kill time and keep me away from video games.

*Ordinary World* is over a million words in total. During the holidays, it took me only a few days to finish it. As a teenager, I had zero interest in the social transformations depicted in it. The only thing that interested me was the love stories among Shaoping, Xiaoxia, Jinbo, and Gu Yangmin. I mistakenly took this book as a romance novel, completely immersed in the love-hate entanglements of a few young people. As it turns out, after rereading it, I found that this accidental misreading wasn't entirely wrong.

## Tragedy
Tragedy deeply shakes the human heart, but I really don't like tragedy, whether it's a tragic plot or a tragic core. Although a happy family reunion is clichéd and bland, at least it still makes me willing to reread it. Once I immerse myself in a tragedy, it takes time to slowly calm my emotions. Knowing the ending of every person and every event before reading breeds an ever-stronger sense of fatalism, which is also one of the reasons I'm unwilling to re-experience tragedy. The tragedy of the Sun brothers makes me unwilling to reread *Ordinary World*. I happened to see this book on a reading app and added it to my bookshelf, but ultimately hesitated for a long time before finally opening it again.

Sun Shao'an rejected Tian Runye because of his simple and narrow-minded thinking—loving each other but unable to be together. In order to suppress his feelings for Runye, Sun Shao'an didn't dare make any response, and even absurdly used a blind date and marriage to forcibly end this relationship.

Sun Shaoping is both similar and dissimilar to his older brother. Shaoping had read more books, seen a bigger world, and dared to face his love for Tian Xiaoxia squarely. But also constrained by the era, I think he didn't seriously consider his feelings for Jin Xiu. There's also Sun Shaoping's inferiority complex—I'm not sure whether it comes from the author himself, as I don't know about Lu Yao's experiences. Shaoping's tragedy comes more from the unpredictability of fate. Besides lamenting the absurdity of fate over Tian Xiaoxia's sudden death, what else can one do?

Of course, there's also the tragedy of that era—thirty years ultimately becoming a moon in the well, a flower in the mist.

## Magical Realism
I didn't know when I was young, but now of course I know this book was created to praise Reform and Opening Up, and it even won the Mao Dun Literature Prize. But the magical realism and deus ex machina in the book emerge one after another. I looked it up on Baidu Baike and it said the author went deep into rural grassroots areas to write the book. I really don't know which parallel universe's rural grassroots that was. I often can't figure out whether this is the author being ironic or genuinely revealing his true feelings.

> Since the land was divided... in just one year, the family no longer worried about not having enough to eat. For farmers, not worrying about food was simply an incredible thing—this was the main goal they had struggled for their entire lives! Once there was food to eat, their most basic demand and most important problem was solved.

The classic "enthusiasm makes people eat their fill"—this could be considered one of the most humorous jokes in the world. Marxist philosophy acknowledges human subjective initiative, but emphasizes that it must function under the premise of respecting objective laws. What truly allowed Chinese people to eat their fill was the high-pressure reactors in chemical plants and hybrid breeding in experimental fields, not the sweat-soaked worn-out spots on farmers' backs. "Working from dawn to dusk, plowing and weeding carefully" had always existed in the traditional agricultural era, yet famines still occurred periodically. If you really want to increase production and income, don't you still need to "apply fertilizer one more time"?

> That day, Tian Si came to Tian Wu with a worried face and said to his younger brother: "Let's go find Shao'an again, see if we can do some odd jobs at his brick kiln? Otherwise, we won't even have money to buy fertilizer for planting wheat in autumn..."
> Everyone had already received a promise from the former team leader that as long as his brick kiln expanded, they could go work there and earn some money they desperately needed. ...Sun Shao'an could completely understand the anxious feelings of these villagers. Now, people had spread all the little fertilizer they had onto the autumn fields, and wheat would be planted around White Dew, but the fertilizer money needed was still nowhere to be found. They had pinned all their hopes on his brick kiln.

Rapid polarization.

> He said that in his sleep he had gone to a cave underground and seen many high officials of the underworld. There was an old man sitting in the main hall wearing reading glasses who was the King of Hell—in front of him was a book of life and death. The King of Hell told him, "In the world of the living, no one in your area manages life and death. I'm calling you down and conferring upon you the title of 'Black Tiger Spirit Official'; when someone is to die, you review it for me first." After receiving the decree, a little ghost even led him on a tour around the underworld... Liu Yusheng was spouting nonsense and rambling, yet it shocked some people in the village into stunned silence... From then on, Pockmarked Liu became a prominent figure in Shuangshui Village. Secretly, people's awe of him had surpassed that of any secular leader in the village.

Feudal superstition making a comeback.

A Communist Party official—give the position to anyone, and they can do it!" Hu Yonghe said arrogantly. Ma Guoxiong actually nodded in agreement with Hu Yonghe's view. Indeed!

## Memories of the First Reading
The last time I read this book was during middle school. Back then, Lu Yao and this novel *The Ordinary World* were so popular that every bookstore in our tiny, no-name county town stocked a copy. During middle school, my dad would occasionally buy me extracurricular reading material. He figured that even if it couldn't improve my Chinese grades, at least it would keep me occupied and away from video games.

*The Ordinary World* is over a million words in total. I finished it in just a few days over a holiday. As a teenager, I had zero interest in the social upheavals depicted in the book. The only thing that captivated me was the love stories among Shao Ping, Xiaoxia, Jin Bo, and Gu Yangmin. I mistook the book for a romance novel, completely absorbed in the emotional entanglements of these young people. As it turns out, after rereading it, I realized that this accidental misreading wasn't entirely wrong.

## Tragedy
Tragedy strikes a deep chord in people, but I genuinely dislike it—whether it's tragic plotlines or a tragic core. While a happy ending may be clichéd and bland, at least it leaves me willing to reread. Once I'm immersed in a tragedy, it takes time to slowly settle my emotions. Knowing the ending of every person and every event before reading breeds an ever-intensifying sense of fatalism, which is one of the reasons I'm unwilling to experience tragedies again. The tragedy of the Sun brothers made me unwilling to reread *The Ordinary World*. I stumbled upon this book in a reading app and added it to my shelf, but ultimately hesitated for a long time before finally opening it again.

Sun Shao'an rejected Tian Runye because of his simple yet narrow-minded thinking—loving each other but unable to be together. To suppress his feelings for Runye, Sun Shao'an didn't dare make any response, even absurdly using a blind date and marriage to forcibly end the relationship.

Sun ShaoPing was both like and unlike his older brother. Shao Ping had read more books and seen a bigger world, daring to face his love for Tian Xiaoxia squarely. But he was also constrained by his era. I think he never seriously considered his feelings for Jin Xiu. And then there was Shao Ping's inferiority complex. I'm not sure if it originated from the author himself—I don't know much about Lu Yao's experiences. Shao Ping's tragedy stems more from the capriciousness of fate. What can you do besides lament the absurdity of destiny when Tian Xiaoxia dies so suddenly?

Of course, there was also the tragedy of that era—thirty years ultimately amounting to nothing but a moon in the well and a flower in the mist.

## Magical Realism
I didn't know it when I was young, but now of course I know this book was written to glorify reform and opening up, and it even won the Mao Dun Literature Prize. But the magical realism and deus ex machina in the book are endless. I looked it up on Baidu Baike and it said the author went deep into rural grassroots communities to write the book. I really don't know which parallel universe's rural grassroots that was. I often can't figure out whether the author is being ironic or genuinely revealing his true feelings.

> Since the land was divided... in just one year, the family no longer worried about going hungry. For farmers, not worrying about food was simply an incredible thing—this was the primary goal they had struggled for their entire lives! Once they had food to eat, their most basic demand and most important problem was solved.

The classic claim that enthusiasm makes people well-fed—this might be one of the most humorous jokes in the world. Marxist philosophy acknowledges human subjective initiative, but emphasizes that it must function on the premise of respecting objective laws. What truly filled Chinese people's stomachs were the high-pressure reactors in chemical plants and the crossbreeding and selection in experimental fields, not the sweat stains worn into farmers' backs. "Rising early and working late, intensive farming"—this had always existed in the traditional agricultural era, yet famines still occurred periodically. If you really wanted to increase production and income, didn't you still need to "apply another round of fertilizer"?

> That day, Tian Si came to Tian Wu with a worried face and said to his younger brother: "Let's go find Shao'an again, see if we can do some odd jobs at his brickworks? Otherwise, we won't even have money to buy fertilizer for planting wheat in autumn..."
> Everyone had already received a promise from the former team leader that as long as his brickworks expanded, they could go work there and earn some money they desperately needed... Sun Shao'an could completely understand the anxiety of these villagers. Now, people had spread all the little fertilizer they had onto the autumn fields, but wheat planting was coming around White Dew, and the money needed for fertilizer was still nowhere to be found. They had pinned all their hopes on his brickworks.

Rapid polarization.

> He said that in his sleep he had gone to a cave underground and seen many high officials of the underworld. There was an old man sitting in the main hall wearing reading glasses—that was the King of Hell—with a book of life and death in front of him. The King of Hell told him, "In the land of the living, no one in your area manages life and death. I'm calling you down and appointing you as the 'Black Tiger Spirit Official'; when someone is about to die, you review it for me first." After receiving the decree, a little ghost even led him on a tour around the underworld... Liu Yusheng was just spouting nonsense, yet it left some people in the village dumbfounded... From then on, Liu Mazi became a prominent figure in Shuangshui Village. Secretly, people's reverence for him had surpassed that for any secular leader in the village.

Feudal superstition making a comeback.

> Judging by his accent, the contractor was from Yuanxi. After a chat, sure enough, he was from Liucha Commune in Yuanxi, named Hu Yongzhou. Shao Ping didn't know that this contractor's younger brother was Hu Yonghe, who had shared a room with his older brother at the Yuanxi County "flaunt your wealth" meeting. Of course, he knew even less that the resourceful Hu brothers had a powerful backer in the region—their cousin Gao Fengge was deputy secretary of the Huangyuan Prefectural Party Committee. That's why these two rural go-getters could travel across prefectures and counties contracting work and doing business with such swagger!
> "I think you could be our county's culture and education bureau chief!" Ma Guoxiong then went on to flatter this "peasant entrepreneur." "Why couldn't I? A Communist Party official—give the position to anyone, and they can do it!" Hu Yonghe said arrogantly. Ma Guoxiong actually nodded in agreement with Hu Yonghe's view. Indeed! Wasn't he himself proof of that?

Corruption through collusion between officials and businessmen.
